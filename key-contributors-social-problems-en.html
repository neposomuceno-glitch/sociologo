Key Contributors to Understanding Social Problems and Their Proposed Solutions

A reference page on notable figures — classic and contemporary — whose work shaped how social problems are understood and what has been proposed to address them. Organized by area of focus. Each entry: their central contribution, the solution or reframing they proposed, a link to a fuller biography, and — where available — a link to a portrait or drawing. See also Selected Works, by Contributor for a matching list of each person's principal publications.

Classical foundations (19th century – early 20th)

  • Karl Marx (1818-1883) — Argued that social problems like poverty and inequality stem from the very structure of capitalism (the relationship between owners and workers), not individual failure. Proposed solution: collective ownership of the means of production, abolishing class division at the root rather than mitigating its symptoms. Biography (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Émile Durkheim (1858-1917) — Introduced anomie (normlessness) to explain social problems such as suicide and crime, showing that they follow social patterns rather than being purely individual. Proposed solution: strengthen social integration and shared moral norms (through institutions like occupational groups) to bind individuals to society. Biography (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Max Weber (1864-1920) — Analyzed how bureaucracy and rationalization, while efficient, could trap individuals in an "iron cage" that erodes meaning and freedom. He proposed no single solution, but his diagnosis shaped later critiques of institutional dehumanization. Biography (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • W.E.B. Du Bois (1868-1963) — Pioneered the empirical sociology of race with The Philadelphia Negro (1899), directly countering biological-racist explanations of Black poverty with data on structural discrimination. Proposed solution: political organization, higher education for a "Talented Tenth," and what became the NAACP's civil rights strategy. Biography (NAACP) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Jane Addams (1860-1935) — Founded Hull House in Chicago (1889), combining direct social work with empirical research on immigrant poverty; in practice founded applied/community sociology. Proposed solution: settlement houses providing education, childcare, and advocacy embedded directly in poor communities. Nobel Peace Prize, 1931. Biography (Nobel Prize) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Robert E. Park and the Chicago School — Developed urban ecology and "social disorganization theory," mapping how neighborhood instability (not individual pathology) produced crime and poverty; foundational to nearly all later urban sociology. Biography (Encyclopedia Britannica)
  • Robert K. Merton (1910-2003) — Extended Durkheim's anomie into "strain theory": people turn to deviance when society promotes goals (e.g., wealth) without equitable access to legitimate means of achieving them. Implication for solutions: expand real access to legitimate opportunity, not just punish deviance. Biography (Columbia University) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • C. Wright Mills (1916-1962) — Coined "the sociological imagination," insisting that personal troubles (e.g., unemployment) must be understood as public issues rooted in social structure, not individual failure. Also identified a "power elite" concentrating decision-making in a small interconnected group. Biography (Encyclopedia Britannica) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Erving Goffman (1922-1982) — Studied stigma and "total institutions" (asylums, prisons) through meticulous observational fieldwork, arguing that institutional labeling compounds harm beyond a person's original condition. His observational case studies influenced deinstitutionalization movements in mental health care, though he treated the underlying medical conditions themselves as real, not as social constructions. Biography (Encyclopedia Britannica) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Howard Becker (1928-2023) — Proposed labeling theory: that society's reaction to an act, not just the act itself, determines whether and how it is treated as deviance, and that severe labeling (especially of young offenders) can itself entrench future criminal behavior. A specific and disputed causal claim about a feedback loop — not a claim that crime or harm itself is unreal — and one several later criminologists challenged empirically. Biography (Northwestern University) · 🖼️ Search for portrait (Wikimedia Commons)

Non-Western and Global South foundations

The classical-foundations section above is entirely Euro-American by convention, not by necessity — sociology as an intellectual practice widely precedes and extends well beyond that lineage. These figures are equally foundational for understanding and addressing social problems, often centuries before or in dialogue with the Durkheim/Weber/Marx canon, rather than derived from it.

  • Ibn Khaldun (1332-1406, Tunisia/North Africa) — Widely recognized by historians of the discipline as sociology's true founder, some 500 years before Comte or Durkheim coined the term. His Muqaddimah (1377) introduced asabiyyah (group solidarity/social cohesion) to explain the rise and fall of dynasties and civilizations — a structural theory of political cohesion and decline that anticipated later sociological concerns with social integration by half a millennium. Biography (Encyclopedia Britannica) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Fei Xiaotong (1910-2005, China) — Trained under Bronisław Malinowski at the London School of Economics; returned to China to found Chinese empirical sociology and anthropology. From the Soil (1947) was a deliberate attempt to build sociological theory from Chinese social organization rather than importing Western frameworks wholesale, contrasting China's relationship-centered social structure with Western individualism. He later became a prominent advocate for rural industrialization policy in the post-Mao economic reforms — an unusual case of a sociologist's theoretical work translating directly into national economic policy. Biography (Chinese Social Sciences Today) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Orlando Fals Borda (1925-2008, Colombia) — Co-founder of participatory action research (PAR), the methodology underlying much of the community research covered elsewhere in this directory (Section 6). Argued that genuine social knowledge about a community's problems must be produced with that community as an active co-researcher, not extracted from it by outside academic experts — a direct challenge to the traditional researcher/subject hierarchy. Biography (Wikipedia) · 🖼️ Search for portrait (Wikimedia Commons)

Race, poverty, and urban inequality (20th – 21st centuries)

  • William Julius Wilson (b. 1935) — Argued in The Truly Disadvantaged (1987) that deindustrialization and the exodus of the Black middle class — not just racism — concentrated poverty in inner cities through job loss and the weakening of local institutions. Proposed solution: large-scale job creation and racially neutral economic reconstruction targeted at inner cities. National Medal of Science; former ASA president. Biography (Encyclopedia Britannica) · 🖼️ Search for portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Elijah Anderson (b. 1943) — Ethnographer of urban life; Code of the Street (1999) documented how, absent trust in police or institutions, informal codes of respect and violence fill the vacuum in poor neighborhoods. Implication: rebuilding a legitimate institutional presence, not just policing, is what actually changes street codes. Biography (Yale University) · 🖼️ Search for portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Loïc Wacquant (b. 1960) — PhD student of William Julius Wilson; extended urban poverty analysis internationally and coined "hyperghetto" and "carceral state" to describe how prisons have effectively replaced other social institutions in managing racialized poverty. Proposed a structural critique of mass incarceration itself as a poverty-management tool. Biography (UC Berkeley) · 🖼️ Search for portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Matthew Desmond (b. 1980) — Evicted (2016, Pulitzer Prize) showed that eviction is not just a symptom of poverty but an active driver of it, through job loss, school disruption, and damaged credit history. Founded the Eviction Lab at Princeton to track eviction data nationwide; proposed a universal housing voucher program as the central policy solution. Biography (Princeton University) · 🖼️ Search for portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Devah Pager (1975-2018) — Pioneered "audit study" methodology, sending matched pairs of job applicants (differing only in race or criminal record) to real employers, producing solid causal evidence of hiring discrimination. Her data directly informed "ban the box" policies removing criminal-history questions from initial job applications. Biography (Harvard University) · 🖼️ Search for portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Herbert Gans (1927-2025) — Argued in "The Uses of Poverty" (1971) that poverty persists partly because it serves latent economic functions for the non-poor (cheap labor, jobs for those who serve or study the poor) — a provocative functionalist-structural critique meant to expose how entrenched interests resist anti-poverty reform. Biography (Columbia University) · 🖼️ Search for portrait (Wikimedia Commons)

Global inequality, development, and world-systems

  • Immanuel Wallerstein (1930-2019) — Developed world-systems theory, arguing that global inequality between "core" and "peripheral" nations is a structural feature of the capitalist world-economy, not a temporary stage nations pass through. See the geopolitical sociology section of the companion directory for the Journal of World-Systems Research that anchors his theoretical framework. Biography (Yale University) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)

Deviance, medicalization, crime, and justice

  • Peter Conrad (1945-2024) — Proposed the sociological concept of medicalization; his 1975 work on ADHD ("The Discovery of Hyperkinesis") argued that defining disruptive behavior as a medical disorder can function as a form of social control, diverting attention from school and family conditions. This is a claim about how a label is applied and by whom, not a claim that the underlying neurological or behavioral conditions are unreal — a distinction the clinical organizations covered alongside his work (Section 8 of the companion directory) explicitly dispute in application, though not always in principle. Biography (Brandeis University) · 🖼️ Search for portrait (Wikimedia Commons)

Gender, intersectionality, and family

  • Patricia Hill Collins (b. 1948) — Developed Black feminist thought and the "matrix of domination," showing how race, gender, and class combine to structure oppression in ways no single axis alone can explain. Former ASA president. Biography (University of Maryland) · 🖼️ Search for portrait (Wikimedia Commons)

Solution-oriented / civic-capital theorists

  • Nan Lin (b. 1938, China/USA) — Developed the leading rival theory of social capital to Putnam's: while Putnam treats social capital as a community-level property (how "civic" a society is as a whole), Lin's Social Capital: A Theory of Social Structure and Action (2001) treats it as a resource embedded in an individual's specific network ties, one that can be deliberately mobilized for jobs, status, or support — explaining, for example, why "who you know" measurably affects getting hired, independent of what you know. Former ASA vice president. Biography (Wikipedia) · 🖼️ Search for portrait (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Pierre Bourdieu (1930-2002) — Developed "cultural capital" and "habitus" to explain how inequality reproduces across generations through unequal access to the tastes, credentials, and networks that schools and employers implicitly reward — not just unequal income. Biography (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)

Digital sociology and the network society

  • Manuel Castells (b. 1942, Spain) — The foundational theorist connecting digital technology to macro-scale social structure. His trilogy The Information Age (1996-98), beginning with The Rise of the Network Society, argued that networks — not individual actors, firms, or even nation-states — have become the basic organizing unit of contemporary society, reconfiguring power, work, and identity around what he called the "space of flows." His theoretical framework is the standard reference point on which the field's own dedicated bodies are built (the BSA's Digital Sociology Study Group, covered in the companion sociology directory, Section 3). He proposed no single "solution" as such, but his diagnosis of networked power directly informs later arguments (from digital-rights advocates to platform-accountability researchers) about where leverage exists to intervene in digital-era inequality. Biography (Wikipedia) · 🖼️ Portrait (Wikimedia Commons)

Notes

  • This list favors figures whose work explicitly diagnosed a social problem and proposed a concrete response — it excludes many important sociological theorists (e.g. Talcott Parsons, Anthony Giddens) whose contributions were more general-theoretical than specific to social problems.
  • The "Classical foundations" section specifically reflects the standard Euro-American sociology curriculum; the separate "Non-Western and Global South foundations" section exists because that standard curriculum genuinely omits centuries of prior and parallel work (Ibn Khaldun precedes Durkheim by about 500 years) — not because non-Western contributions are a minor or later addition to the field.
  • Several figures here (Conrad, Wallerstein, Fals Borda, Castells) overlap with the companion file sociology-websites-directory-en.html, where their associated organizations, journals, and institutional homes are covered in more depth — this page is about the people and their ideas, that one is about where to find the ongoing work.
  • Biography links are a mix of university faculty pages, Nobel Prize biographies, and encyclopedia entries — chosen for reliability and durability rather than convenience; a Wikipedia link would also work for most of these names if you prefer a single consistent source type.
  • On portraits: for well-established, widely documented figures, the portrait link goes directly to a Wikimedia Commons category page with content. For less universally documented contemporary scholars, the link instead runs a live Commons image search on their name — this avoids linking to a category page that might not exist under that exact name, at the cost of an occasional unrelated result with the same name appearing; verify the specific image before using it. Historical figures predating photography (Ibn Khaldun) are shown via later artistic depictions, not photographs.
  • A handful of entries here (Becker, Conrad, and to a lesser extent Goffman) draw on the social-constructionist tradition, which studies how labels and categories are socially applied — that is a narrower, more specific claim about classification, not a claim that the underlying problems (crime, illness, disability) are unreal. This page treats those cases as one interpretive perspective among several, not as a dominant framework; most entries here are structural, empirical, or materialist in orientation (audit studies, world-systems analysis, ethnographic and quantitative fieldwork on poverty and housing), and that balance is intentional.
  • This remains a representative sample, not an exhaustive one — entire traditions (Indian subaltern studies, African indigenous sociology through figures like Akinsola Akiwowo, Latin American dependency theory through sociologists Fernando Henrique Cardoso and Enzo Faletto) could each support a list of comparable size on their own; let me know which region or tradition you'd like expanded and I can go deeper on it specifically.
