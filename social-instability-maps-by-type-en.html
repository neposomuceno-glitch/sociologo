Social Instability Maps, by Problem Type

Twelve separate maps — one per dimension — rather than a single combined score, since a country can rank severely on one axis (e.g. organized crime) while being unremarkable on another (e.g. famine risk). Each tab uses that dimension's own published metric and its own color thresholds.

Mapping the social world does something a table of numbers can't: it reveals spatial relationships — clustering, contagion, and proximity — that shape social problems as much as any single country's own statistics do. A famine or a conflict rarely stays within one border; it moves across neighboring territory, along trade routes, or through a river basin, and a map makes that adjacency visible in a way an ordered list never will. Geography also carries explanatory weight of its own: coastlines, chokepoints like the Strait of Hormuz, mountain ranges that shelter insurgencies, or the pull of a regional labor market all help explain why instability concentrates where it does, not just that it does. And since most of the crises mapped here are compound — a country in conflict often also faces famine, disease, and displacement at the same time — seeing them overlaid geographically, rather than buried in separate tables, is what makes that pileup visible. That's the working assumption behind treating each dimension as its own map instead of a single composite score: a problem's geography is often as informative as its severity.

Metric: two complementary, established monitors — the Open Doors World Watch List (focused on anti-Christian persecution) and USCIRF's Countries of Particular Concern (CPC) / Special Watch List (covering all religions, including treatment of Muslim minorities like the Uyghurs and Rohingya). Both are standard references used across the field, including by governments and UN bodies.

Red = Open Doors "extreme persecution" level (top 15) and/or a USCIRF CPC designation. Orange = Open Doors ranks 16-30 and/or USCIRF Special Watch List. Yellow = specific, documented restrictions or incidents that don't rise to a top-tier designation on either list.

A note on scope: neither Ukraine nor Israel appears on the Open Doors list or on USCIRF's CPC/SWL lists — the two most-used religious-freedom monitors don't rank them alongside North Korea, Afghanistan, or China. Both have real, documented religious-freedom disputes, covered separately below rather than folded into the main red/orange/yellow scale, since doing so would overstate what the primary sources actually say.

A note on method: advocacy-group indices (Open Doors) and government commissions (USCIRF) aggregate refugee testimony, field reports, and journalism precisely because independent reporting is often impossible within the worst-rated countries (there's no free press operating inside North Korea to verify Open Doors' figures). Where direct investigative journalism exists that complicates an index's framing — as with Nigeria below — it is cited alongside the index rather than omitted.

Documented disputes, outside the standard ratings:

Ukraine — Ukrainian Orthodox Church (Moscow Patriarchate). Ukraine's 2024 law bans religious organizations whose governing center is located in a state "conducting armed aggression" against Ukraine, targeting the UOC-MP for its historical ties to the Russian Orthodox Church, which backed the invasion. The government and many Ukrainians frame this as a national-security measure against a church used for foreign influence; some UOC leaders, the UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission, and UN special experts have called it a restriction on religious freedom. USCIRF expressed concern about the law but also stated that "Russia remains the deepest threat to religious freedom in Ukraine," including repression in Russian-occupied Ukrainian territory. Neither Ukraine nor Russia appears on USCIRF's CPC list for this specific dispute; Russia is a CPC for separate, broader reasons.

Israel, the West Bank, and Gaza — access to and war damage at religious sites. US State Department International Religious Freedom reports document: periodic Israeli restrictions on Muslim access to the Ibrahimi Mosque/Tomb of the Patriarchs and the call to prayer there; attacks and vandalism against Christian clergy and holy sites in Jerusalem (71-105 documented incidents per year in recent reports, with a low conviction rate); and, during the Israel-Hamas war, Israeli strikes that killed Muslims and Christians sheltering in mosques and churches in Gaza and damaged dozens of religious sites. The Israeli government states these are security measures and disputes characterizations of intent. Palestinian Authority restrictions on some unrecognized religious groups are also documented in the same reports. Israel does not appear on USCIRF's CPC or SWL lists.

Why separate maps: a composite score hides which specific problem is driving a country's instability. South Africa, for example, is a global outlier in inequality and organized crime, but doesn't appear at all on the conflict or famine maps — a single combined score would have obscured that distinction.

Snapshot, not live data: conflict and famine data change week to week (see the maps-links subpage for live trackers); inequality and organized-crime scores update annually or less often.

On disputed cases: ratings reflect what the cited indices report, not a political stance.
